Tornado Health: a member portal, designed and built with AI, with a human in the loop

A 22-page health plan portal, accessible and data-driven, built in about 20 hours by directing an AI coding agent.

A concept project, not client work. Tornado Health, its members and its data are made up.

Overview

Tornado Health is a demo member portal for a fictional health plan: sign in, see coverage and where you stand on your deductible, follow claims and EOBs, find and book care, estimate a cost, carry your ID cards, message the plan, appeal a denial and manage your account. I was the designer and the director: I set the rules, made every design call, reviewed every screen and pushed back until it was right. Claude Code, an AI coding agent, wrote the code.

The problem

Health plan portals tend to answer the plan's questions instead of the member's. Members come with a few urgent ones: Am I covered? What do I owe, and why? Where should I go, and what will it cost? They get dense tables, insurance terms and dead ends. And people using screen readers, keyboards or small phones often get the worst of it.

What I set out to do

  • One clear job per page, with the most urgent task first, not an FYI.
  • Plain language and real numbers: "$860 left on your deductible," not a percentage.
  • WCAG 2.2 AA from the start, not as a final pass.
  • No dead space and no bulky heroes; every section in a container, nothing floating.
  • A real system: one stylesheet of tokens, one script, shared navigation, data in JSON files.

Key decisions

Navigation edited in one place

The megamenus, account menu, footer and phone navigation started as copies in every page. I asked for one source. Each page now holds two empty placeholders that load the shared header and footer, and the current section lights up from the page's own heading. Adding a link to a menu adds it on phones too, because the phone menu is built from the megamenus.

ID cards at their actual size

A digital ID card gets held up to a front-desk window. I asked for true proportions first, then actual size: 3.375 × 2.125 inches on any screen wide enough, with flip, print (front and back, card-sized) and reorder, and a full-screen view on phones.

Costs from where you stand

The cost estimate compares in-network places using the member's real deductible and out-of-pocket progress. For Roy, a knee MRI costs $760 at one place and $924 at another, and the page shows exactly why: deductible first, then 20%, capped at the maximum.

Nothing jumps while it loads

Measuring on a throttled connection showed the claim page shifting badly as data arrived (a layout shift score of 0.45). Placeholders now match what loads into them, and pages built entirely from data wait invisibly behind one card-shaped placeholder. Every page measured now scores under 0.01.

Accessibility found in the details

Automated checks pass, but some problems only showed up by looking closer: checkbox and radio outlines at 1.8:1 contrast (now 5:1), a focus ring drawn around a whole panel on arrival, an arrow centered on capital letters next to lowercase text, a phone number showing for a 24/7 line that was really weekdays only. Each was fixed at the system level, so every page got it.

Less where it isn't needed

Not every idea stayed. The AI Chat promo came off most pages (the chat button is already everywhere); per-answer "Was this helpful?" buttons were overkill; a side column on the Help center went full width. Saying no was as much of the work as saying yes.

How I worked with AI

I worked in short, direct prompts, 158 of them, and 71 were corrections or additions sent while the agent was still working. Every prompt is published, exactly as I typed it. (Text I pasted in, like the opening spec, is left out.)

  • Rules that stick: my design preferences (no borders on rounded shapes, solid active states, no em dashes, images never bigger than the logo) were saved as standing rules, so I stopped repeating myself.
  • A living spec: every decision went into DESIGN-RULES.md, about 490 lines by the end.
  • Review agents: three reviewers (structure, design system, accessibility), switched off during small edits and run at checkpoints. They checked Home and Coverage along the way, then the whole site at the end.
  • A cleanup before the final review: an audit for unused and duplicate code. It removed dead styles and an unused color token, merged five copies of the form-error code into one, turned three look-alike panel classes into one, merged about a dozen rules that set the same thing, and cut the script by 34 lines. Then every check ran again to prove nothing looked or worked differently.
  • The final review: the three agents went over all 22 pages and only reported; I approved their fixes as one list. They found what the automatic checks can't: checkboxes, radios, switches and selects showed no focus ring; the skip link was hidden under the sticky header; tabbing could hide controls under the header and the phone bottom bar; one table scrolled the page sideways at 320px; and a bug took two buttons out of the Tab order. Also some Bootstrap blue left over, dividers drawn too dark, off-scale padding, and copies of the same helper. All fixed, then every check ran again.
  • Proof, not promises: after each change, automated checks in a real browser: accessibility (axe), HTML validation, layout shift, sideways scrolling and the actual flows (sign in, order a card, send an appeal).

How it was tested

Every check below ran in a real browser after each change, and all of them again after the final cleanup.

  • Accessibility, WCAG 2.2 AA

    0 violations

    axe-core 4.14.1 (every WCAG 2.0, 2.1 and 2.2 A and AA rule, plus best practices) in a real browser, Microsoft Edge 158. All 22 pages at 375px and 1280px, and in their states: form errors, open menus and dialogs, the phone menu, flipped ID cards, search results and empty states, sent messages, each appeal step.

  • What axe can't see

    Fixed and re-measured

    Scripted measurements: checkbox and radio outlines (1.8:1 contrast, now 5:1), where focus lands after every action, no focus ring drawn on arrival, and the optical centering of arrows, badges and counters, measured in pixels.

  • Valid HTML

    0 errors

    html-validate 8.18.2, standard rules, on all 22 pages, both shared partials, and each page as rendered in the browser.

  • Consistent structure

    0 problems

    A script checks every page: the same head (Bootstrap and Font Awesome from a CDN with integrity hashes), one h1, landmarks, heading order, unique ids, no inline styles, no !important.

  • Links

    0 broken

    Every link and #section jump on all pages and in the shared header and footer, checked against real files and ids.

  • Layout shift while loading

    Under 0.01 everywhere

    On a slowed connection (150ms latency, about 1.6 Mbps, 4× slower CPU) with an empty cache, every page at 320, 375, 768 and 1280px. Worst: 0.008 (Claims, 320px). Before the fixes: up to 0.45.

  • Small screens and zoom

    No sideways scrolling

    Every page at 375px and 1280px; most also at 320, 768, 1024 and 1440px. 320px wide is the same as 400% zoom on a laptop.

  • Real tasks

    All pass, no errors

    Scripted in the browser: sign in and out, search for care and book a visit, estimate a cost, flip, print and order ID cards, read, reply to and write messages, clear notifications, edit contact info and address, switch preferences, send an appeal, enroll in a program, reset the demo, and the 404 page at a deep address.

  • Code cleanup

    No visible change

    An audit for unused and duplicate code, then every check above run again.

  • Speed (Lighthouse)

    Mobile 86

    On the home page, earlier in the build: Accessibility 100 and Best Practices 100 on desktop and mobile; Performance 96 on desktop, 86 on mobile (main content in 3.3 seconds, mostly the shared libraries).

  • Review agents

    All fixes applied

    Three reviewers (structure, design system, accessibility) checked Home and Coverage at checkpoints, then all 22 pages in a final pass. Every fix they proposed was applied, then every check above ran again.

Not tested yet:

  • With screen readers (NVDA, VoiceOver) and by people who use them.
  • In Safari and Firefox, and on real phones. Testing used Edge (Chromium) at phone and desktop sizes.
  • Text-only zoom to 200% and Windows high contrast mode.
  • With members, in usability sessions.
  • A Level Access scan of the whole site.

Results

  • 22 pages and a live design system, from 11 data files, one stylesheet and one script.
  • No accessibility violations (axe, WCAG 2.2 AA) on every portal page at phone and desktop widths, in their interactive states too.
  • Valid HTML on every page and both shared partials.
  • No layout shift above 0.01 on any page, on a slow connection with an empty cache.
  • No sideways scrolling from 320px phones up.

What I'd do next

  • Test it with people. This was designed from experience and heuristics, not research. Usability sessions with members, including screen reader users, come first.
  • Speed. On phones the main content takes about 3.2 seconds to appear, mostly from shared libraries; that's worth tuning.
  • Pharmacy. Refills and drug costs are the biggest member task the demo leaves out.

Taking it to production: Angular and AEM

If this became a real portal on Angular and Adobe Experience Manager, the deciding line is simple: AEM owns content; Angular and an API layer own member data. Health information never goes into AEM. AEM runs headless, with authors editing in Universal Editor, and Angular reads its content through saved GraphQL queries.

A taste of the code

Claims for the member being viewedReplaces the claims page module and claims.json 
@Injectable({ providedIn: 'root' })
export class ClaimsService {
  private http = inject(HttpClient);
  private member = inject(MemberContextService);

  readonly claims = toSignal(
    toObservable(this.member.viewingId).pipe(
      switchMap(id => this.http.get<Claim[]>(`/api/members/${id}/claims`))
    ),
    { initialValue: [] }
  );
}

What it would take

Roughly 4 to 6 months with 4 to 6 people (a rough estimate): two Angular developers, one AEM developer, one backend developer for the API layer, a designer, and part-time QA and accessibility. The prototype shortens it, because the design rules, components, copy, edge cases and accessibility patterns are already decided and written down.

The real risks aren't front-end work:

  • Getting at the plan's systems: claims and member data, through FHIR or older internal services.
  • HIPAA: a business associate agreement with each vendor, plus logging and data handling.
  • Identity: signing members in, and how family members can see each other's information.
  • Price data: real price-transparency files for the cost estimate.
