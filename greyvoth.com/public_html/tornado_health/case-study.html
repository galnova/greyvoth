Tornado Health: a member portal, designed and built with AI

A 20-page health plan portal, accessible and data-driven, built in one day by directing an AI coding agent.

A concept project, not client work. Tornado Health, its members and its data are made up.

Overview

Tornado Health is a demo member portal for a fictional health plan: sign in, see coverage and where you stand on your deductible, follow claims and EOBs, find and book care, estimate a cost, carry your ID cards, message the plan, appeal a denial and manage your account. I was the designer and the director: I set the rules, made every design call, reviewed every screen and pushed back until it was right. Claude Code, an AI coding agent, wrote the code.

The problem

Health plan portals tend to answer the plan's questions instead of the member's. Members come with a few urgent ones: Am I covered? What do I owe, and why? Where should I go, and what will it cost? They get dense tables, insurance terms and dead ends. And people using screen readers, keyboards or small phones often get the worst of it.

What I set out to do

  • One clear job per page, with the most urgent task first, not an FYI.
  • Plain language and real numbers: "$860 left on your deductible," not a percentage.
  • WCAG 2.2 AA from the start, not as a final pass.
  • No dead space and no bulky heroes; every section in a container, nothing floating.
  • A real system: one stylesheet of tokens, one script, shared navigation, data in JSON files.

Key decisions

Navigation edited in one place

The megamenus, account menu, footer and phone navigation started as copies in every page. I asked for one source. Each page now holds two empty placeholders that load the shared header and footer, and the current section lights up from the page's own heading. Adding a link to a menu adds it on phones too, because the phone menu is built from the megamenus.

ID cards at their actual size

A digital ID card gets held up to a front-desk window. I asked for true proportions first, then actual size: 3.375 × 2.125 inches on any screen wide enough, with flip, print (front and back, card-sized) and reorder, and a full-screen view on phones.

Costs from where you stand

The cost estimate compares in-network places using the member's real deductible and out-of-pocket progress. For Roy, a knee MRI costs $760 at one place and $924 at another, and the page shows exactly why: deductible first, then 20%, capped at the maximum.

Nothing jumps while it loads

Measuring on a throttled connection showed the claim page shifting badly as data arrived (a layout shift score of 0.45). Placeholders now match what loads into them, and pages built entirely from data wait invisibly behind one card-shaped placeholder. Every page measured now scores under 0.01.

Accessibility found in the details

Automated checks pass, but some problems only showed up by looking closer: checkbox and radio outlines at 1.8:1 contrast (now 5:1), a focus ring drawn around a whole panel on arrival, an arrow centered on capital letters next to lowercase text, a phone number showing for a 24/7 line that was really weekdays only. Each was fixed at the system level, so every page got it.

Less where it isn't needed

Not every idea stayed. The AI Chat promo came off most pages (the chat button is already everywhere); per-answer "Was this helpful?" buttons were overkill; a side column on the Help center went full width. Saying no was as much of the work as saying yes.

How I worked with AI

I worked in short, direct prompts, 158 of them, and 71 were corrections or additions sent while the agent was still working. Every prompt is published, exactly as I typed it. (Text I pasted in, like the opening spec, is left out.)

  • Rules that stick: my design preferences (no borders on rounded shapes, solid active states, no em dashes, images never bigger than the logo) were saved as standing rules, so I stopped repeating myself.
  • A living spec: every decision went into DESIGN-RULES.md, about 490 lines by the end.
  • Review agents: three reviewers (structure, design system, accessibility), switched off during small edits and run at checkpoints.
  • Proof, not promises: after each change, automated checks in a real browser: accessibility (axe), HTML validation, layout shift, sideways scrolling and the actual flows (sign in, order a card, send an appeal).

Results

  • 20 pages and a live design system, from 11 data files, one stylesheet and one script.
  • No accessibility violations (axe, WCAG 2.2 AA) on every portal page at phone and desktop widths, in their interactive states too.
  • Valid HTML on every page and both shared partials.
  • No layout shift above 0.01 on any page measured, on a slow connection with an empty cache.
  • No sideways scrolling from 320px phones up.

What I'd do next

  • Test it with people. This was designed from experience and heuristics, not research. Usability sessions with members, including screen reader users, come first.
  • Speed. On phones the main content takes about 3.2 seconds to appear, mostly from shared libraries; that's worth tuning.
  • Pharmacy. Refills and drug costs are the biggest member task the demo leaves out.
